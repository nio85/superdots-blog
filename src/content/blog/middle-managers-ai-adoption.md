Maria Rossi — a composite drawn from conversations with managers across Italy, Germany, and the UK over the past year — manages a team of eight analysts at a logistics firm in Milan.

In November, her CEO announced that the company would be "AI-first by Q2." No one asked Maria what that meant for her team's existing workflows. No one explained which of the six tools being piloted across the organization her analysts were expected to use. Her manager told her to "drive adoption." Her analysts asked her which data was safe to put into an AI system and which wasn't.

She didn't know the answer.

Maria is not unusual. Variations of this story are playing out in companies across Europe and North America — in manufacturing, financial services, retail, professional services, and healthcare. The tools have arrived. The mandate has been announced. The organizational infrastructure to make adoption actually work hasn't been built.

This is not a story about technology failing. It is a story about a gap in how organizations are deploying it — a gap that sits precisely at the middle layer of every large company, and that no one is talking about honestly.

## The Layer Nobody Mentions

When organizations talk about AI transformation, they describe two groups.

They describe the executives who set the strategy — the people who read McKinsey reports, attend Davos panels, and sign off on vendor contracts. They describe the frontline workers who will use the tools — the ones whose jobs might change, whose tasks will be automated, whose skills need updating.

The middle manager sits between both. She is the person who has to translate the executive mandate into something her team can actually do on Tuesday morning. She is the person her team brings their questions and anxieties to. She is the one who manages the performance reviews, coordinates the workflows, resolves the conflicts between the new tool and the existing process, and absorbs the friction when the rollout goes sideways.

She is the one who has to tell her team that yes, this tool is now required, while privately being uncertain whether it actually improves anything.

And she is almost never the person anyone designs the [change program](/blog/ai-change-management) for.

## The Squeeze

McKinsey's management-layer surveys on AI adoption reveal a consistent pattern: middle managers are being asked to drive implementation while being given less authority and fewer resources to do so than previous transformation programs required. The 2024 edition of [McKinsey's Global Survey on AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2024) found that while executive confidence in AI strategy was rising, frontline and middle-management confidence in their organizations' ability to manage the human side of AI change remained flat.

The specific bind is structural.

Above her, the executive has made a commitment — to the board, to investors, to the quarterly earnings call. That commitment tends to be expressed in terms of adoption rates, efficiency targets, or cost reductions. It has a timeline. It has a number attached to it.

Below her, the frontline team has questions that are reasonable and unanswered. Which tasks should they use AI for? What happens to the time saved? If the AI produces an error, who is accountable? Will their job change? Will there be fewer of them?

Maria is in the middle. She has been told what the destination is. She has not been given the authority to redesign the route.

This is what makes the middle-manager problem structurally different from the challenge facing executives or frontline workers. The executive can set the goal and measure the outcome. The frontline worker can learn a new tool and adapt her daily practice. The middle manager has to coordinate between a goal she didn't set and a team she can't redesign the workflows for — because workflow redesign requires cross-functional authority, system access, and budget she typically doesn't have.

The result, in many organizations, is that AI tools get added on top of [existing workflows](/blog/ai-workflow-automation) rather than replacing them. The analyst still does the work the old way. Then she reviews the AI output. Then she reconciles the two. The workload increases. The time the executive said would be freed up doesn't materialize, because no one redesigned the workflow to actually create that time.

This is not what the executive announced at the all-hands. It is, however, what the manager deals with on Monday morning.

What makes this particularly difficult is that the middle manager typically can't escalate the problem without it sounding like resistance. Saying "the AI rollout is adding to my team's workload" in a culture where leadership has publicly committed to AI-first transformation is politically difficult. The safer move is to absorb the friction quietly and keep adoption numbers moving in the right direction. So the real implementation experience stays invisible, and the gap between announced outcomes and operational reality widens.

## The Organizational Failure Behind the Numbers

Boston Consulting Group's [research on enterprise AI adoption](https://www.bcg.com/press/24october2024-ai-adoption-in-2024-74-of-companies-struggle-to-achieve-and-scale-value) consistently finds that the majority of the ROI gap in AI deployments isn't technical — it's organizational. Tools work. Implementation structures don't. People don't know what to do differently, and the people best positioned to tell them — middle managers — have been given the mandate but not the means. BCG estimates that around 70% of the gap between projected and realized ROI from AI investments is organizational and behavioral, not technical.

BCG's finding is often cited as evidence that change management matters. What gets less attention is the specific structure of the problem: it's the management layer, specifically, that carries the implementation burden while having the least clarity about what success looks like. The executive has a target. The frontline employee has a task. The manager has both — and has to build the bridge between them with tools and authority she wasn't given.

Deloitte's Global Human Capital Trends research on management fatigue in technology transformations adds another dimension. Transformation programs accumulate. An organization that spent three years on cloud migration, then two years on digital workflow tools, and is now running an AI implementation isn't asking its middle managers to handle one change. It's asking them to handle the cognitive and emotional load of continuous transformation — while continuing to manage their teams' daily output.

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data on manager wellbeing showed increasing burnout rates specifically among managers, not frontline workers. The group absorbing multiple simultaneous pressures tends to show the strain.

## The Skills Gap No One Is Filling

The World Economic Forum's [Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/) identified a set of skills that would be increasingly valuable for managers navigating AI at work: critical evaluation of AI outputs, workflow redesign, change facilitation, and cross-functional coordination.

These are not skills that most managers were hired for or trained in. Most middle managers built their careers on domain expertise and execution — understanding the work their team does and making sure it gets done well. The meta-skills of change architecture and technology evaluation are a different category.

They are also not the skills that most AI adoption programs train managers in. The typical [AI training rollout](/blog/ai-employee-training) focuses on tool use — how to write a prompt, how to use the interface, how to interpret outputs. This is useful. It is also insufficient.

What managers actually need is something harder to teach and harder to measure: the judgment to decide which parts of a workflow should be automated and which shouldn't, the communication skills to explain that judgment to both the team above and the executive above them, and the organizational authority to actually implement those decisions. The judgment call — this task is a good fit for AI, that one isn't, here's why — requires deep understanding of the work itself, the team's capabilities, and the quality standards the output needs to meet.

Most companies are providing the first kind of training. Almost none are providing the other two, because they require time, protected experimentation space, and organizational trust that most transformation programs don't build in.

The gap between what the WEF identifies as needed and what organizations are actually providing is not small. It is the gap between a training program and a change in how management roles are designed.

## The Irony in the Architecture

There is something almost circular about the position middle managers find themselves in.

AI, deployed thoughtfully, could significantly reduce the coordination overhead that defines much of middle management work. Scheduling, status updates, information routing, exception handling, progress tracking across a team — a meaningful share of what a manager does every week is exactly the kind of structured, repetitive, information-processing task that current AI tools handle well. If AI were being deployed to reduce management coordination overhead first, it might actually create the conditions for more considered transformation work.

But most AI deployments as they are currently structured don't reduce that coordination work. They add to it. Someone has to supervise the AI outputs. Someone has to check them for quality and accuracy. Someone has to handle the edge cases that the tool doesn't cover — and there are more edge cases than the demo suggested. Someone has to explain to the team why the process changed, absorb the frustration when the tool doesn't work as expected, and then explain to the executive why the adoption numbers aren't tracking to the projection.

That person is usually the middle manager.

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data on manager burnout is relevant here. The 2024 edition showed managers reporting higher rates of stress and lower rates of engagement than either executives or individual contributors — the group absorbing the largest number of simultaneous demands showing the highest strain. That pattern predates AI. The AI deployment wave is landing on an already overloaded layer.

The technology that was supposed to create breathing room has, in its current deployment model, often created more pressure on exactly the layer of the organization that needed it most.

## What Organizations Are Getting Wrong

The failure isn't in the tools. It's in the architecture of the rollout.

Organizations are deploying AI as a technology initiative — managed by IT, measured by procurement, evaluated by the finance team against a cost model. The organizational change dimension, which is where most of the implementation friction actually lives, is treated as a communication problem. You send the all-hands announcement. You train people on the tool. You measure adoption rates at the aggregate level.

What you don't typically do is give middle managers the authority and the time to actually redesign the workflows that the AI is supposed to improve. You don't create space for them to experiment, to fail, to iterate. You don't ask them what their team's specific obstacles are and then give them resources to address those obstacles. You don't protect their time from the normal output expectations while they're also supposed to be leading implementation.

The asymmetry between what organizations ask of middle managers in AI rollouts and what they give them to do it is striking once you start looking for it. The manager is expected to drive adoption, field her team's questions, manage performance through the disruption, report upward on progress, and handle the exceptions when the tools don't work as promised — all while maintaining the team's existing output. No organization would structure a product launch this way. Somehow AI transformation gets structured exactly this way, at scale, across industry.

The organizations that are seeing real results from AI adoption tend to have figured out a different model. They treat the middle manager as the implementation unit, not just the communication channel. They give her authority over workflow decisions within her domain. They protect structured time for redesign — not just training hours, but actual space to evaluate what's working and change what isn't. They measure the quality of implementation, not just the quantity of adoption.

This is harder to announce at a board meeting. It doesn't produce a clean slide with a percentage and an arrow. But it's what the evidence from adoption programs, consistently and across sectors, points toward.

## The Question That Doesn't Have a Clean Answer

Maria, the logistics analyst manager in Milan, figured out her own approach eventually.

She got permission — quietly, informally — to run a pilot with two of her analysts for six weeks. They chose two specific task types where AI assistance seemed most promising: categorizing incoming freight exception reports and drafting first-response emails to carrier queries. They documented what actually happened: what worked, what created more work than it saved, what questions the tool produced that required human judgment to answer. At the end of six weeks, she had a real picture of what AI adoption looked like in her team's context — not the vendor's context, not the executive's model, but the actual workflow experience of eight people doing a specific kind of logistics work.

She shared it with her manager. Her manager shared it with the executive team. A few of her findings made it into the next quarter's AI rollout guidance. The exception categorization workflow got redesigned. The email drafting tool got scoped more narrowly. Two of her analysts became informal advisors to other teams going through the same process.

This is one story, from one manager, at one company. It worked because she had the organizational safety to run the experiment, a manager who trusted her, and an executive team that was willing to learn from implementation experience rather than only measure adoption percentages.

The structural problem remains for most organizations. They have not yet figured out how to build those conditions at scale — how to give middle managers the protected time, the clear authority, and the psychological safety to run honest implementation pilots and report back what they actually found. They are still deploying AI as a top-down technology rollout, measuring adoption at the tool level, and wondering why the business impact numbers don't match the projections.

The middle manager's problem isn't going away. The layer that has to translate AI strategy into AI practice is the same layer that is least supported to do it. That's not because organizations don't care about managers — most would say they do. It's because the transformation programs are designed at the executive level, where the view of what "AI adoption" means is different from the view at the middle. Until that gap is acknowledged and addressed structurally, the distance between what organizations announce about AI and what actually happens inside them will remain wide.

Whether that changes depends less on the technology than on whether organizations are willing to redesign how they run transformation programs. That is, in the end, a management problem — not an AI problem.
